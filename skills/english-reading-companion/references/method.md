# Method Reference

Use this reference when identifying subtle meaning, resolving overlap among sections, or checking whether an analysis is sufficiently deep.

## Beyond the Words diagnostic

For each important phrase or sentence, ask:

1. Would a literal Chinese translation sound correct but miss the intended meaning?
2. Does the expression depend on situation, relationship, register, or shared cultural knowledge?
3. Does later information change how an earlier sentence should be understood?
4. Is the writer using understatement, irony, euphemism, humor, tenderness, or emotional restraint?
5. Does an ordinary word carry a special role in this story?
6. Would a learner understand every word yet still miss why the sentence matters?

If any answer is yes, the item belongs in Beyond the Words or deserves equivalent contextual explanation.

## Canonical examples from the project

### `let him go`

Do not stop at “让他走”. In a life-or-death scene, it can mean accepting that rescue has failed and ceasing attempts to hold on. The emotional meaning comes from context, not the dictionary senses of *let*, *him*, and *go*.

### `in good company`

Do not treat it as physically being with pleasant people. It reassures someone that many respected or capable people share the same difficulty or trait; therefore they are not alone or disqualified by it.

### `complete with ...`

Do not reduce it to “完整地带有”. It often presents a detail as the finishing touch that makes a scene, role, or image fully recognizable. Depending on context, it can sound vivid, amused, theatrical, or mildly ironic.

### `wear the pants`

Recognize the idiom about holding authority in a household. If a story also involves literal trousers or taking over a role, explain the wordplay and the reinterpretation.

### `take on a whole new meaning`

Explain the retrospective shift: later events cause a familiar phrase or earlier detail to acquire a different significance. This often links Story and Thinking, not only Language.

### `bring a smile to one's face`

Explain the natural causal expression and its emotional tone. Do not analyze *bring* as physical movement.

## Layer boundaries

| Layer | Main question | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Story | What happened, and how did the meaning unfold? | sequence, reveal, reversal, ending |
| Language | How is the meaning packaged naturally? | chunks, collocations, syntax, rhythm |
| Thinking | What viewpoint or mental model shapes the piece? | contrast, reframing, value judgment, theme |
| Beyond the Words | What would literal comprehension miss? | implication, tone, idiom, subtext, redefinition |

Allow one item to connect multiple layers, but explain the connection instead of repeating the same observation under several headings.

## Section ownership

Assign every core insight one primary section for full explanation. Other sections may mention it only to perform their own distinct function.

| Section | Explain fully here | Do not repeat here |
|---|---|---|
| Story Flow | event sequence, narrative turn, and what changes | full expression or subtext analysis |
| Memorable Line | why the line was selected and what role it plays | a second full summary of the theme |
| Key Expressions | concise contextual meaning, tone, transfer value, and optional example | deep subtext already assigned to Beyond the Words |
| Beyond the Words | non-literal meaning, implication, tone, relationship, and contextual redefinition | story summary already established in First Reading |
| Language Patterns / Writing Style | what formal choice creates rhythm, contrast, suspense, compression, or emotional effect | the higher-level thematic conclusion |
| Deep Understanding | the viewpoint or theme emerging from earlier findings | renewed vocabulary, grammar, or sentence analysis |

Use these rules when an item belongs to several layers:

1. Choose the section where the insight's main learning value belongs.
2. Explain it fully only there.
3. In another section, add a brief reference only when needed for coherence.
4. Keep the reference to one sentence unless it adds a genuinely different layer.
5. Before finishing, remove paragraphs whose deletion would lose no unique information.

## Depth calibration

- **Simple article:** concise story summary, 3 expressions, one strong Beyond the Words observation, theme, takeaway, reflection. For a concentrated short reversal, use the core path rather than separate overlapping sections.
- **Fragmented or rhythm-led article:** identify 2–4 structural anchors that organize the reading, then explain only the language points that those anchors make worth noticing.
- **Language-dense article:** add paragraph breakdown and up to 5 expressions.
- **Structurally difficult sentence:** add a Sentence Workshop with meaning chunks, grammatical spine, modifiers, contextual paraphrase, and why the construction works.
- **Emotionally subtle or sensitive article:** spend more space on narrative restraint, retrospective meaning, and tone than on grammar; name fact, author interpretation, and reader association only where their boundary is needed.

## Conditional calibration patterns

### Concentrated short reversal

When an ending, irony, or repeated pattern carries nearly all of a short text's learning value, preserve the story and decisive contextual explanation first. Merge adjacent sections that would only restate the same turn. Brevity is not a reason to omit the explanation that makes the turn intelligible.

### Sensitive autobiographical boundary

Keep three levels separate when the text invites overreach: **Textual fact** (what happened or was said), **Author interpretation** (the meaning the writer gives the event), and **Reader association** (a possible response that the source does not establish as fact). Use this distinction briefly and only where needed; it prevents unsupported diagnosis, destiny, causation, compensation, or “the pain was solved” claims without flattening emotion.

### Role-identity gateway

A role word is a gateway when it changes how readers understand later actions, objects, relationships, or expectations. Give the minimum stable context and return to the source. Do not promote an ordinary role word merely to fill an expression slot.

### Structural anchors

For rhythm-led or fragmented writing, select two to four formal anchors—such as time markers, milestones, repeated wording, direct address, dashes, ellipses, short-sentence runs, or modal shifts—and explain their combined movement. Do not map a simple linear narrative or turn anchors into a grammar inventory.

## Sentence Workshop pattern

Use only when needed:

1. Quote the target sentence.
2. Divide it into natural meaning chunks.
3. State the grammatical spine in one line.
4. Attach each modifier to what it modifies.
5. Paraphrase the whole meaning naturally in context.
6. Explain the author's stylistic choice.
7. Give an alternative only if the contrast teaches something useful.

## Common failure modes

- Starting with vocabulary before the reader understands the event.
- Translating every sentence and calling it analysis.
- Selecting too many expressions of low transfer value.
- Treating Beyond the Words as a list of formal idioms only.
- Explaining dictionary meaning without tone or context.
- Giving a generic moral unsupported by the story.
- Repeating the same theme in several sections.
- Fully re-explaining one core insight in Story Flow, Memorable Line, Beyond the Words, Writing Style, and Deep Understanding.
- Overexplaining grammar that does not affect comprehension or style.
- Missing how the final sentence reinterprets the title or earlier details.
